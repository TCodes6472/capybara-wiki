02

Biology & body design

The capybara is a giant cavy in the family Caviidae. Its chunky, barrel-shaped body is built for life around water. The eyes, ears and nostrils sit high on the head, allowing it to keep much of its body submerged while still watching and breathing.

03

Social life & behavior

Capybaras are famously social. They commonly live in groups and communicate with whistles, barks, purr-like sounds, clicks and other vocalizations. Grooming and close physical contact help maintain group bonds.

“If there is water nearby, there is probably a capybara considering a bath.”

They can be active at different times depending on temperature and human disturbance. During hot periods, water and shade become especially important.

04

Habitat

Native to much of South America, capybaras favor habitats close to water: wetlands, riverbanks, marshes, flooded grasslands and forest edges.

05

Diet

They are herbivores. Their menu is dominated by grasses and aquatic or semi-aquatic vegetation, with other plant material eaten when available.

Fun detail: their digestive system includes fermentation in the hindgut, helping them extract nutrients from fibrous plants.

What makes capybaras special?

01

🏊 Semi-aquatic pros

They swim well and can use water as a refuge from heat and danger.

02

👀 Built-in lookout

High-set eyes, ears and nostrils make shallow-water surveillance easier.

03

🤝 Social by design

Group living gives them many opportunities for communication, grooming and vigilance.

04

🦷 Rodent superpowers

Their incisors keep growing, so chewing tough vegetation also helps wear them down.

07

Baby capybaras

Young capybaras are precocial: they are born well developed, with fur, open eyes and the ability to move around soon after birth. They can begin sampling solid food early while still nursing.

08

Predators & threats

Depending on location, wild capybaras may face predators such as jaguars, pumas, caimans, anacondas and large raptors, especially when young.

09

Conservation

Capybara populations are affected locally by habitat loss, hunting and changes to wetlands and waterways. Their conservation is closely connected to protecting the freshwater and wetland ecosystems they depend on.
